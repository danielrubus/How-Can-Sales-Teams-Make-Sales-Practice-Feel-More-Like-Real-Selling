# How Can Sales Teams Make Sales Practice Feel More Like Real Selling

## The problem with most roleplay is not that reps dislike practice. It is that they can tell when the exercise is fake.

The buyer had already said no twice.

Then the manager playing the customer smiled and said, "Okay, give me your best response."

The rep paused.

Everyone in the room knew what was happening.

This was roleplay.

The buyer was going to cooperate.

The rep was going to get another chance.

The manager would give feedback.

Then everyone would move on to the next exercise.

I have sat through enough sales training sessions to know what usually happens next. The rep improves because the scenario becomes predictable. The manager gets encouraged because the second attempt sounds better. The score goes up.

Then the rep gets on a real customer call three days later.

The customer says something completely different.

The improvement disappears.

That is the problem I would attack first.

**Sales practice should feel slightly uncomfortable.**

Not humiliating. Not theatrical. Not designed to make the rep fail.

Just unpredictable enough that memorizing the expected answer stops working.

There is good reason to take that distinction seriously. Research on sales training has found that spaced practice can improve the quality of transfer into the job, sales competence and certain key figures compared with massed practice. More recent research on AI roleplay, using data from almost 2,000 salespeople at a large U.S. industrial company, found an average performance improvement of about 2%, with larger effects for some groups than others. The researchers also explicitly point to the need to manage transfer into real selling rather than assuming simulation performance is enough. 

That last part is the interesting one.

Simulation is not the destination.

## The perfect roleplay is usually a bad roleplay

A good sales roleplay should contain enough uncertainty to force the rep to think.

That sounds simple.

It changes the entire exercise.

Imagine a rep is practicing a price objection.

The traditional scenario says:

"Your competitor is cheaper. What would you say?"

The rep has probably seen this objection before.

They know the exercise is about price.

They know they are expected to defend value.

They produce the answer.

Now change it.

The buyer says:

 "We actually like your product more. The problem is that procurement will reject this price."

That is a different conversation.

The objection is not really product value.

It is internal approval.

The rep now has to decide whether to defend the price, ask about the approval process, explore the business case, or understand what procurement will actually challenge.

That decision is the practice.

Not the memorized response.

## I learned this after watching a rep fail the same scenario perfectly

One of the strangest coaching sessions I watched involved a rep who was exceptionally good at roleplay.

He knew the framework.

He used the right language.

He asked the recommended questions.

His manager loved his performance.

Then we changed one sentence.

Instead of saying, "Your price is too high," the buyer said:

"I can probably get this approved, but I don't think the CFO will see enough difference from what we have."

The rep immediately started explaining product features.

The framework disappeared.

The manager stopped the exercise.

"Why did you go there?"

The rep looked genuinely confused.

"Because he said the CFO doesn't see the difference."

Exactly.

The rep was listening to the surface language rather than diagnosing the buying problem underneath it.

That was the useful failure.

We would not have found it if the roleplay had stayed predictable.

## Real customers do not give you the objection you prepared for

This is one reason traditional objection-handling libraries have a ceiling.

They teach categories.

Customers create variations.

A price objection can mean:

"We don't have budget."

"We have budget but not for this."

"Your competitor is cheaper."

"I don't believe the ROI."

"My manager will not approve this."

"We already have a solution."

"We are not convinced switching is worth the disruption."

"I need procurement involved."

Those are not the same problem.

A rep who has memorized eight price responses may still struggle with the ninth version.

The practice environment should therefore vary the reason behind the objection.

Otherwise the rep is learning the answer rather than the skill.

## Research gives us another reason to change the design

A 2002 study examining sales roleplay training found that preparing trainees before the roleplay could improve training efficiency and performance outcomes. The researchers specifically looked at roleplay enhancers and found that preparation helped trainees get into the exercise and improved performance. 

That is worth paying attention to because "make roleplay realistic" is sometimes interpreted as "throw the rep into the deep end."

I would not do that.

Realism does not mean randomness.

The rep needs enough context to understand the situation.

They should know who the buyer is.

They should understand the account.

They should know what stage the opportunity is in.

Then the conversation should become uncertain.

That combination is much closer to the real job.

## The buyer should have a reason for saying what they say

This is another detail that separates useful practice from theater.

A simulated buyer should not randomly throw objections at a rep.

The buyer should have an internal reason.

Suppose the scenario says:

"The buyer objects to price."

That is weak.

Instead:

"The buyer's department has already committed budget to another initiative. They believe your product is more capable, but they do not yet see why switching this quarter justifies moving money."

Now the rep has something to discover.

If they ask:

"What makes the timing difficult?"

the buyer can answer.

If they ask:

"Is the concern the amount itself or the need to justify the change internally?"

the buyer can reveal more.

The conversation starts behaving like a conversation.

That is what we want.

## Practice should get harder after the rep gets better

There is another mistake I see frequently.

The rep performs well.

The manager says, "Great, you've got it."

The scenario ends.

I would do the opposite.

Make the next scenario harder.

If the rep can handle a straightforward price objection, introduce a buyer who agrees with the value but refuses to change vendors.

If they can handle that, introduce a buyer with an existing contract.

Then introduce an executive who has only five minutes.

Then make the executive skeptical.

The objective is not to make the rep miserable.

It is to discover the boundary of the skill.

Where does performance break?

That is the coaching opportunity.

## There should be a moment when the rep does not know what to say

I would actually design for this.

Not every time.

But often enough.

If the rep never gets stuck, the practice is probably too easy.

The useful moment is what happens next.

Does the rep panic?

Do they start talking?

Do they ask a question?

Do they admit they need clarification?

Do they recover?

Do they invent an answer?

That last one is particularly important.

Salespeople sometimes feel pressure to answer everything immediately.

Real confidence includes knowing when not to guess.

A practice environment gives you a safe place to build that behavior.

## The best roleplay feedback is painfully specific

"Good job."

Not useful.

"Be more confident."

Usually not useful.

"Build more rapport."

Too vague.

Try this instead:

"You answered the buyer's concern before finding out what they meant by expensive."

That gives the rep something to change.

Or:

"You heard the procurement objection and immediately moved into product differentiation. Ask one question about the approval process before you defend the product."

Now the rep has a next attempt.

This matters because feedback without another attempt is often just commentary.

The rep needs to use the feedback immediately.

Then the coach can see whether the behavior changed.

## I would use the same scenario twice, then change it

There is a useful rhythm here.

First attempt.

Feedback.

Second attempt.

Feedback.

Third attempt with a changed buyer.

That third attempt tells you something the first two do not.

Did the rep learn the behavior?

Or did they simply memorize the correction?

Suppose the first buyer says:

"Your price is too high."

The manager teaches the rep to ask a clarifying question.

The rep does it correctly on the second attempt.

Now change the buyer.

"Your price isn't the issue. I don't think we can justify the switching cost."

If the rep asks another useful question, the skill may be transferring.

If they immediately go back to defending price, the rep learned the response rather than the principle.

That is an extremely useful distinction.

## Spaced practice makes this easier to build

Sales training research gives us another reason not to cram all practice into one workshop.

In a field study of 64 bank employees, spaced practice produced better transfer quality, higher self-reported sales competence and improved key figures than massed practice, although it did not produce higher transfer quantity. 

The study does not tell us that every sales organization should practice at one exact interval.

It does support the idea that learning should return over time.

That makes sense operationally.

Practice today.

Use the behavior tomorrow.

Practice again next week.

Introduce a variation.

Then see what survives.

The rep gets multiple chances to retrieve the skill after some time has passed.

That is more interesting than one three-hour roleplay marathon.

## AI changes who gets to practice

Human roleplay has always had a scheduling problem.

The manager is busy.

The peer is busy.

The rep needs practice now.

That is where AI has a practical advantage.

The rep can start a scenario without waiting for another person.

That matters especially for small moments.

A rep has a difficult customer call tomorrow.

They want ten minutes of practice.

They do not need a formal workshop.

They need a buyer.

They need the scenario.

## But AI realism has to be earned

I would be careful here.

An AI buyer that always responds politely is not realistic.

An AI buyer that throws random objections at the rep is not realistic either.

The simulation needs a coherent buyer.

The buyer should have priorities.

The buyer should have information they are willing to share and information they are not.

The buyer should react to what the rep says.

If the rep asks a good question, the buyer should reveal something useful.

If the rep gives a bad answer, the buyer should become less convinced.

That is what creates a conversation rather than a quiz.

The latest field research on AI roleplay is encouraging but still early. The 2026 study of nearly 2,000 salespeople found a positive average treatment effect of about 2%, while also finding substantial differences depending on salesperson and supervisor characteristics. 
That is exactly the kind of evidence I prefer.

Positive result.

Limited claim.

Known variation.

Still more to learn.

## The manager should use AI practice data as evidence, not as a verdict

Suppose Practis shows that a rep repeatedly struggles with executive discovery.

The manager should not simply say:

"Your score is low. Practice more."

They should listen.

Maybe the rep is asking technically correct questions but not connecting them to business impact.

Maybe the scenarios are unfamiliar.

Maybe the rep understands the skill but becomes rushed when the buyer interrupts.

Maybe the score is exposing a different problem.

The manager's job is interpretation.

The platform can make the evidence easier to collect.

It should not remove judgment from the process.

That distinction becomes more important as AI-generated feedback gets better.

## One thing I would never simulate

I would not try to reproduce every possible customer conversation.

That becomes an impossible project.

The goal is not coverage of every sentence a buyer might say.

The goal is coverage of the **decisions the rep needs to make**.

Can they diagnose an objection?

Can they ask a useful follow-up?

Can they avoid discounting prematurely?

Can they handle uncertainty?

Can they redirect a conversation?

Can they maintain discovery when the buyer wants to jump to price?

Can they recover after making a mistake?

Those are transferable skills.

The words surrounding them can change.

## The best scenario may be based on a real lost deal

This is where sales organizations have an advantage they often ignore.

They already have thousands of real conversations.

Use them.

Take a lost opportunity.

Remove the customer identity.

Rebuild the situation.

Give the rep the information the salesperson had at the time.

Then recreate the difficult moment.

Do not tell the rep how the original call ended.

Let them make the decision.

That creates practice from an actual business failure.

It also gives the manager a much better conversation afterward.

"Here is what the customer actually said."

"Here is what happened next."

"Here is where we lost control."

"Try it again."

That is better than inventing another generic objection.

## One of our best practice sessions ended badly

A rep was practicing with a buyer who kept refusing to answer discovery questions.

After about six minutes, the rep became frustrated.

"I can't sell to someone who won't tell me anything."

The manager smiled.

"That's the scenario."

The rep laughed.

Then they tried again.

This time, instead of asking another discovery question, they summarized what they knew and asked the buyer whether they had misunderstood the priority.

The buyer finally opened up.

That was the moment the rep learned something.

Not how to answer an objection.

How to keep a conversation alive when the buyer is not helping.

That is a real sales skill.

## The test I would run over the next two quarters

I would make one prediction:

**Sales teams that move from predictable roleplay toward varied, buyer-specific practice should see stronger transfer of targeted conversational behaviors within two quarters.**

That is testable.

Take a group of reps.

Measure one behavior before practice.

Use realistic scenarios with different buyer motivations.

Space the practice over time.

Track performance inside practice.

Then sample real customer conversations.

Do not stop at the practice score.

If the behavior improves in simulation but not in live calls, the problem is transfer.

If it improves in both, you have stronger evidence.

If neither improves, change the practice design.

That is a much more useful experiment than asking reps whether they enjoyed the training.

## What makes practice feel real is not the technology

This is the part I would be careful about.

A fancy simulator does not automatically create realistic practice.

A bad scenario remains bad whether it is delivered by a manager, a video or an AI agent.

The realism comes from the structure.

A buyer with a reason.

A conversation with uncertainty.

A rep who has to make decisions.

Feedback tied to behavior.

Another attempt.

A harder variation.

Then a connection to the real sales call.

Practis can make that process easier to run at scale.

The technology matters.

The design matters more.

## The real measure is what happens when the buyer goes off-script

That is the point I would leave sales leaders with.

Do not ask whether your reps can perform the roleplay.

Ask what happens when the roleplay stops behaving like the script.

That is where selling starts.

The buyer interrupts.

The executive changes the subject.

Procurement appears early.

The champion suddenly becomes uncertain.

The CFO asks for the number.

The customer says they are happy with the competitor.

The rep does not know the answer.

A good practice system should make those moments familiar before they become expensive.

That is the opportunity for [Practis](https://www.practis.ai/).

Not to make sales practice look impressive.

To make the first difficult customer conversation feel a little less like the first time.
